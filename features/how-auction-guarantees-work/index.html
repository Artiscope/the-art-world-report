How Auction Guarantees Work

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

At the big evening sales in New York and London, many of the most expensive lots already have a buyer lined up before the bidding starts. That promise is called a guarantee. This is how guarantees work, who pays for them, and why parts of the trade are uneasy about them.

The basic deal

A guarantee is a promise to the seller of a minimum price. The figure is agreed privately and is not published. In the simplest version, if the work fails to sell, the auction house pays the seller the agreed sum and keeps the work. In return the house expects a bigger share of the sale, either a higher seller's commission or a cut of any price above the guarantee.

For the seller this removes the risk of a public failure. For the auction house it is a way of winning the consignment against a rival. If the work sells well, the house and any backer share in the profit, though the seller may have to give away some of the upside. If it does not sell, the house owns a work it has already paid for.

Sharing the risk with outside backers

Most big guarantees are now shared with outside backers. Christie's calls the arrangement a third-party guarantee. Sotheby's introduced its own version, the irrevocable bid, in 2009. The Art Newspaper described the two in 2018 as essentially the same thing.

The backer agrees in advance to bid up to a set level. If someone else bids more, the backer is paid for having taken the risk. According to Artnet News, that payment can be a set fee, a share of the amount above the guarantee, or both. If nobody bids more, the backer buys the work. A backer who wins can also pay less than other bidders would have, because the fee is subtracted from the final price.

The share of the upside is a matter for negotiation. The Art Newspaper put a typical figure at 20% to 30% of the amount above the guarantee in 2018, with deals ranging from 10% to 50%. In 2025, insiders quoted by Apollo put it at 15% to 20%.

Backers can also win the lot they guaranteed, and that can come with a discount. Mari-Claudia Jiménez, co-head of the art law practice at Withers and a former Sotheby's chair, told Apollo that "Christie's pioneered the idea that if you were the guarantor you would always get a discount." Artnet News reported that when a first printing of the US Constitution sold at Sotheby's in November 2021, Sotheby's had found an irrevocable bidder at the last minute: the hedge fund manager Kenneth Griffin, who won the lot. The price was first given as $47.4m with fees and later revised down to $43.2m, and Artnet concluded that Griffin had received a $4.2m discount. As Artnet explains, auction houses "legally have to report net prices whenever the buyer receives a financing fee".

How buyers can tell

Guaranteed lots are marked, though the marks vary between houses and have changed over time. In 2011 The Art Newspaper listed the symbols then in use. Christie's used a circle for its own guarantee, and both a circle and a diamond for a lot backed by a third party. Sotheby's used a horseshoe for an irrevocable bid, and an underlined v to show that someone with an interest in the lot might be bidding. In 2025 Apollo described a Canaletto at Christie's in London whose online listing carried a diamond, showing a third-party guarantee.

Christie's relies on announcements instead. Jennifer Zatorski, then its international commercial director, told The Art Newspaper in 2011: "Announcements are made that someone with a financial interest will be bidding." Now that catalogues are mostly online, Citi points out, buyers have to listen to the list of lots the auctioneer reads at the start of the sale. Even that list does not say whether a third party is paying a reduced buyer's premium.

The rules have been tested. Christie's has cited New York City law, which says an auctioneer's interest in a lot, "direct or indirect", must be disclosed. In 2015 The Art Newspaper reported that at least five works in Christie's New York sale on 13 May had outside backers that the catalogue did not show. Christie's first left those works off a list it supplied, then said they were "subject to an in-house guarantee, as noted in the catalogue", and that it had met its legal obligations. The lawyer Frank Lord of Herrick Feinstein told the paper: "It would seem like the auction house would have to disclose that."

A short history

How long guarantees have existed is, in Apollo's words, "a matter of debate". Philip Hook, a former director of Impressionist and modern art at Sotheby's, says the house guaranteed £35,000 for Poussin's The Adoration of the Shepherds in 1956 and lost £6,000 on it. The Art Newspaper, citing an unnamed source, dates the first guarantees to 1971, when Sotheby's offered a group of Kandinskys and other works from the Guggenheim with a guaranteed minimum. Apollo says guarantees stayed rare until the art market boom of the early 2000s.

The most expensive early lesson came from Phillips. Bernard Arnault bought the house in 1999 and used large guarantees to compete. The Art Newspaper says the 2001 Smooke sale carried a guarantee of over $180m and raised $86m. Apollo gives $85m against a rumoured $170m. Arnault sold the firm in 2002 (Apollo says a majority stake) after losses of about $400m.

The boom that followed ran on guarantees. According to Noah Horowitz's book Art of the Deal, cited by The Art Newspaper, Sotheby's issued $902m of guarantees in 2007, up from $131m in 2005. When the market turned, some deals went badly wrong. Apollo cites a Wall Street Journal estimate that by the end of 2008 Christie's and Sotheby's held about $63m of art they had been obliged to buy. Guarantees all but disappeared between 2008 and 2010.

They came back with the market. Before Christie's New York sale in May 2015, 50 of the 85 works were marked as guaranteed, 36 by the house and 14 by third parties. In May 2018 Modigliani's Nu couché sold at Sotheby's New York for $157.2m (The Art Newspaper does not say whether that figure includes fees). The Nahmad family had reportedly given it the highest third-party guarantee then on record, at $150m.

How common they are now

Guarantees are concentrated at the very top of the market. Ben Gore, Christie's chief operating officer, told Apollo that only 1.5% of the house's lots carry a guarantee. By value the picture is different. Lindsay Dewar of the research firm ArtTactic told Apollo that nearly 73% of lots by hammer value in the post-war and contemporary evening sales at Christie's, Sotheby's and Phillips were guaranteed in the first half of 2025. Apollo gives 72% for Impressionist and modern art. Artnet News, citing ArtTactic, reported that guaranteed lots made up 83% of the total value of the November 2021 New York sales, a record.

A fully guaranteed sale does not mean strong bidding. At the Emily Fisher Landau sale at Sotheby's in November 2023, all 31 evening lots were guaranteed, and Apollo says 13 of them sold at or below the low estimate. Insiders told Apollo that margins at the top of the market can be as low as 2%.

The arguments for and against

Supporters say guarantees do what they claim to do: protect the seller. The dealer Helly Nahmad told The Art Newspaper in 2011 that guarantees "are like an insurance". Artnet News takes the comparison a step further: "If the auction guarantee is the insurance for the consignor, the irrevocable bid is the insurance for the auction house." In 2018 Adam Chinn, then Sotheby's chief operating officer, said: "I don't think there's a conflict of interest."

Critics worry about what bidders cannot see. In the same 2018 article, Harry Smith of the art advisory firm Gurr Johns said: "Guarantees have the potential to be the next big art-market scandal". The lawyer Rebecca Foden of Boodle Hatfield said they "distort the market and inflate prices". The economist Olav Velthuis, speaking in 2015 about the push for openness, said "you want more transparency—but this is resulting in the opposite." Felix Salmon, chief financial correspondent at Axios, has described heavily guaranteed auctions as "more like gussied-up private sales".

What it means

For anyone reading auction results, a guarantee changes what the hammer price tells you. When most of a sale's value was guaranteed, much of the result was agreed in private before the auction began. A lot that sells at or near its guarantee, possibly to its own backer at a net price, says less about demand than a lot that several people competed for. The symbols and the auctioneer's opening announcements are the only public clues, which is why they are worth paying attention to.
