Auction Houses

What Auction Results Mean for Emerging Artists

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • Which number are you looking at?
  • Who gets the money
  • What a high price does next
  • Why galleries worry
  • How to read a result
  • Sources

A strong auction price looks like good news for a young artist. It is often the least reliable number in their career, and they rarely see any of the money.

Most of what an emerging artist's work sells for is private. Galleries rarely publish prices, and private sales between collectors are not reported. The exception is auction. When a work sells at auction, the result is published and logged in price databases, where it stays.

That makes auction results the figures everyone quotes. It also makes them easy to misread. This article sets out what an auction result does and does not tell you about an artist at the start of their career.

Which number are you looking at?

An auction produces more than one price. The hammer price is the winning bid. The buyer then pays a fee on top, the buyer's premium, which goes to the auction house and not to the seller.

The premium is large enough to change the picture. Each house sets its own rates, usually in bands that fall as the price rises. Three examples in sterling show the range. Bonhams charges 30% on the first £25,000 of the hammer price for sales after 1 October 2026. Christie's charges 28% up to £1.5 million under a schedule effective from 1 September 2026. Tate Ward's terms for buyers set 25% plus VAT on the hammer price up to £500,000, and 12% plus VAT on any part above that.

Taking 25% as an example, a work that hammers at £10,000 costs the buyer £12,500, before any tax due on the premium.

Published results often include the premium, so it is worth checking which figure a report is using. The seller receives less than the lower one, because the house also takes a commission from them. When two results are compared, the first thing to check is whether both are quoted the same way.

Who gets the money

Almost never the artist. A work at auction is being sold by someone who already owns it. In a report on quick resales, Artnet News found that artists and their galleries rarely gain directly when a work is resold. Voluntary payments from sellers do happen, but they are uncommon.

In the UK there is one legal exception. Under the Artist's Resale Right, an artist is owed a royalty when a work is resold through an auction house or dealer for £1,000 or more. The rate is 4% on the portion up to £50,000 and falls in steps above that, with a cap of £12,500 on any one sale. It is collected and paid out by collecting societies such as DACS.

So a painting resold for £10,000 earns its maker £400. That is real money for an artist early in their career, but it is a small share of the price. The United States has no equivalent: Artnet reports that federal resale royalty bills have failed at least eight times since 1978.

What a high price does next

A record result can affect more than one sale. Owners of similar work learn what it might fetch, and buyers who could not get work from the gallery can try the saleroom instead.

The scale of this in recent years was unusual. Auction sales of works by artists born after 1974, offered at auction within three years of being made, rose from $22.8 million in 2012 to $257.4 million in 2021, according to Artnet.

Then it reversed. The research firm ArtTactic found that the auction market for artists under 40 fell 43% in 2023 and a further 53% in the first half of 2024, as reported by Anny Shaw in The Art Newspaper. The paper gave examples. Flora Yukhnovich's auction sales fell 88% in 2023 against the year before, and Njideka Akunyili Crosby's fell 72%, Salman Toor's 68% and Avery Singer's 63%. Artnet, which defines the category differently, recorded a 31.3% fall in the first half of 2025, against a fall of 8.8% for fine art auctions overall.

The paintings did not get worse between 2021 and 2023. The number of people bidding for them changed. An auction price measures demand in one room on one day, and for a young artist with few works in circulation, two determined bidders are enough to set a record.

Why galleries worry

The concern for galleries is what happens to their own prices. During the boom, some raised their primary prices after inflated auction results. One collector quoted by The Art Newspaper called those increases unsustainable.

This is why galleries try to keep new work out of the saleroom. Sale contracts that restrict resale have become standard, Anna Brady reported in The Art Newspaper in 2023. Typical terms bar resale for three to five years or give the gallery first refusal.

Whether those clauses would stand up is uncertain. Lawyers quoted in the same report disagree, and the adviser Sibylle Rochat notes that they have not been tested in UK courts. In practice galleries rely on something simpler: a blacklist. A buyer who resells too soon risks losing access to the gallery for good.

How to read a result

For anyone looking at an emerging artist's auction record, a few questions do most of the work.

  • Hammer or with fees? Compare like with like.
  • How does it sit against the estimate? A result far above estimate can mean strong demand, or a cautious estimate.
  • How many results are there? One sale is an event. A pattern over several years is information.
  • How old was the work? A piece resold within a year or two of leaving the studio says more about speculation than about lasting demand.
  • What else is on the record? Museum collections, a consistent gallery and steady exhibitions tell you more about a career than any single price.

Auction results are the most visible numbers in the art market, and for established artists with long sales histories they are useful. For artists at the start, they are best read as one piece of evidence among several, and usually not the strongest one.
