How Public Art Activations Generate Attention

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • Four kinds of public art event
  • Why it works
  • How good are the numbers?
  • When a brand is paying
  • What it means for an artist
  • Sources

Art in a public place can draw a crowd that no gallery could hold. Working out what that attention is worth, and to whom, is harder than the headline numbers suggest.

In August 2024 a new work by Banksy appeared somewhere in London every day for nine days. A goat on a wall in the west, elephants in Chelsea, a gorilla lifting the entrance gate at London Zoo. Fans learned of each one through the artist's own website and Instagram account, the Associated Press reported.

Fans rushed to see each one. So did other people. A wolf painted on a satellite dish was carried off by masked men. A cat on a billboard was taken down by contractors while onlookers booed. The zoo put its mural behind an acrylic shield, watched by three security guards. One specialist told the AP that the public reaction might itself be part of the work.

The series was covered by news outlets around the world, the AP among them. Marketers call this kind of thing an activation. This article looks at how it works when the plan is deliberate.

Four kinds of public art event

The word covers several different things, and they have different owners.

The civic commission. A public body pays for a work in a prominent place. A well-known British example is the Fourth Plinth in Trafalgar Square, where a new sculpture is installed every two years. The current work, Lady in Blue by Tschabalala Self, was unveiled in September 2026 as the 16th commission. The programme is funded by the Mayor of London with support from Arts Council England and Bloomberg Philanthropies.

The spectacle. A single large work, shown briefly. In February 2005 Christo and Jeanne-Claude placed 7,503 fabric gates along the paths of Central Park in New York for 16 days. More than four million people came, according to the Central Park Conservancy. The artists had first proposed it in the 1970s.

The trail. Dozens of decorated sculptures are placed around a town for a few months, then auctioned for charity. In Brighton and Hove, a trail of painted dog sculptures drew more than 350,000 visitors and raised £310,000 for Martlets Hospice in autumn 2016, organisers reported.

The sponsored wall. A brand or property developer pays an artist to paint a mural. This is the kind most argued over, as discussed below.

Why it works

The examples share a few features, and they are worth separating from the art itself.

  • It is free and in the way. Nobody has to decide to visit. The audience is whoever is passing.
  • It ends. Sixteen days, a few months, or until someone removes it. A deadline gives people a reason to go now.
  • It photographs. Each visitor's picture is another small advertisement.
  • It can be argued about. Online, people debated what the London animals meant for days, the AP noted.

How good are the numbers?

Public art events come with impressive figures attached. They deserve a closer look.

The Central Park Conservancy credits The Gates with an estimated $254 million in economic activity for New York. Its anniversary press release does not say who calculated that figure or how.

The Brighton trail was credited with adding £10 million to the local economy, along with 14.6 million social media impressions. Those statistics were commissioned by Wild in Art, which organised the trail with the hospice.

That does not make them wrong. But visitor counts for a free outdoor event are estimates, and an economic impact figure depends on assumptions about what people would otherwise have spent. Two academics who studied sculpture trails, John Thompson and John Day, wrote in the journal Local Economy that various claims are made about the economic, social and artistic impact of such trails, and that some people walk or drive past the exhibits without ever seeing them.

A fair reading is that public art reliably produces attention, and that the conversion of attention into money is much less well measured.

When a brand is paying

The sponsored mural raises a different question: is it art or an advertisement?

At least one city has drawn a legal line. Los Angeles adopted rules in 2013 banning commercial messages in murals, and in 2014 a mural carrying a beer logo was removed for breaching them, Christine MacDonald reported for In These Times.

Brands have adapted by leaving the logo off. MacDonald describes one mural paid for by a drinks company that carries no reference to it at all. The company promoted a film of the painting process instead.

Artists are divided. Some, MacDonald writes, worry that sponsored work blurs the line between art and advertising and makes it harder to find walls that have not been paid for. Others pointed out that corporate commissions pay far more than community mural grants, and artists need to earn a living.

What it means for an artist

Attention is not the same as income. Two of the London animals, the wolf and the cat, were taken down on the day they appeared, one by masked men and one by contractors.

For an artist offered a public commission, the practical questions are familiar ones.

  • Who is paying, and will the audience know?
  • How long will the work stay, and who decides when it goes?
  • Who owns it afterwards, and who may reproduce the image?
  • Is the fee for the work, or is the exposure being offered in place of one?

A public work can put an artist in front of far more people than a gallery exhibition. Whether that becomes a career depends on what happens next, and on the slower work described in our feature on how galleries build careers.
