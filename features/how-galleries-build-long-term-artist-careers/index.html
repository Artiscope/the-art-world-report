How Galleries Build Long-Term Artist Careers

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

A gallery's job is often described as selling art. The harder part is deciding what not to sell, to whom, and how quickly.

When a gallery agrees to represent an artist, very little is exchanged at first. No money changes hands up front, and many galleries work from a consignment agreement for the works they hold instead of a full contract. What begins is a working arrangement that both sides expect to last for years, and in the best cases for decades. Sadie Coles, who opened her London gallery in 1997, has represented Sarah Lucas and John Currin for almost 30 years.

That length of time is the point. A single sold-out exhibition proves that there is demand this season. A career is something else: steady prices, work in public collections, and buyers who are still there when fashion moves on. This article looks at how galleries try to build the second thing, and why it has become harder to do.

What the commission pays for

The usual arrangement is consignment. The artist lends work to the gallery, the gallery sells it, and the two share the price. An even split remains common, though it is a convention and not a fixed rule. Discounts and tax normally come off the top before the money is divided.

In return the gallery carries the costs of selling: the space, the staff, the opening, the shipping, the fairs. Those costs are rising. The Art Basel and UBS Global Art Market Report 2026 found that dealers' operating costs rose by about 5% on average in 2025, while total dealer sales rose 2% to $34.8 billion. Art fairs alone accounted for 35% of dealer turnover.

The work has also widened. Coles describes representation today as "much more multilayered" than it was. It can include managing an artist's archive, producing digital material and funding large fabrication projects. Stefan Benchoam of Proyectos Ultravioleta in Guatemala City says his gallery is the first point of contact for visiting curators from biennials and major museums.

Pricing slowly

The first tool a gallery has is price, and the long-term approach is to use it sparingly. Prices are set by where an artist is in their career and what comparable work has sold for, with the aim of steady progression over time. Gallery prices then become the reference point for anything that later appears at auction.

The reason for caution is that art prices are hard to reverse. A 2025 Artnet report by Kate Brown quotes the Zurich adviser Thomas Stauffer warning that once prices are too high they are difficult to lower. In the same report the dealer Sebastian Gladstone says he keeps prices conservative and is more interested in an artist's fourth or fifth exhibition than the first.

That discipline is under strain. Brown reports that the price ceiling for work described as "emerging" has moved from about $10,000 to roughly $30,000 to $45,000.

Placement: choosing the buyer

The second tool is choosing who buys. In most markets the highest offer wins. In the primary art market, dealers often prefer the buyer who will do most for the artist's standing.

The writer Tim Schneider calls this a matching market, where seller and buyer both have to agree. In an excerpt from his book The Great Reframing, he gives the example of a gallerist choosing a $75,000 sale to the Guggenheim over a $200,000 sale to an unknown buyer. The museum sale pays less today. It adds a line to the artist's record that supports every later price.

Waiting lists come from the same thinking. Schneider credits the New York dealer Mary Boone with popularising them in the 1980s, and notes that a collector's standing often counts for more than their place in the queue. Museums are commonly offered a discount, because a public collection means long-term care and the chance of exhibition.

Why the model is under pressure

All of this is slow, and it costs money before it earns any. That is manageable only if the gallery is still representing the artist when the work pays off.

Often it is not. Smaller galleries have complained for more than a decade that larger ones sign their artists once sales begin. The Art Newspaper reported in 2013 that the growth of the largest galleries was squeezing smaller operators. Brown's 2025 report describes the next stage: the biggest galleries now sign artists much earlier, so the traditional climb from small gallery to large one is being compressed. Pam Evelyn joined Pace in 2023 at the age of 27. Sasha Gordon joined David Zwirner in September 2024.

The figures show where the strain falls. In 2025, dealers turning over less than $500,000 saw double-digit growth in average sales, and those above $10 million grew 3%. Dealers in the middle, with turnover between $1 million and $10 million, fell by 1%. These are the galleries that traditionally carried artists through the middle years of a career.

Shared representation

One response is for galleries to share artists. In November 2023 Hauser & Wirth began partnering with younger and smaller galleries to represent emerging artists together. The stated benefit is that the artist can keep working at a comfortable scale and the smaller gallery stays in business.

Not everyone is convinced. Dealers quoted in the Artnet report say shared arrangements can blur who is responsible for what, and that the smaller partner tends to lose leverage. Others are changing how they work with artists directly. The London gallerist Rose Easton holds regular meetings with her artists about sales and the health of the gallery, and has questioned whether the standard 50% commission should be the default.

What it means

For an artist, the useful question about a gallery is less how quickly it can sell and more what it turns down: which prices, which buyers, which opportunities that arrive too early. For a collector, a gallery that prices carefully and places work with institutions is doing the things that tend to hold value.

For the industry, the open question is who pays for the slow years. Linda Pyke, a director at THK Gallery in Cape Town, describes relationships between galleries and emerging artists as built over decades. The market data suggest that the businesses best placed to wait that long are also the ones under the most financial pressure.
