What It Costs a Gallery to Show at an Art Fair

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • Why galleries go
  • The stand
  • Everything else
  • Who loses money
  • How fairs have responded
  • What it means
  • Sources

Art fairs now bring in a large share of what many galleries sell. They are also expensive, and the price of the stand is only part of the bill.

Why galleries go

The number of fairs has grown quickly. Citing the 2015 TEFAF art market report, Artnet News says there were 55 fairs worldwide in 2000 and at least 180 by 2014. Writing in February 2026, Tim Schneider wrote that there are now "hundreds of fairs per year".

For dealers, fairs are where much of the selling happens. The Art Basel and UBS Global Art Market Report 2026 found that art fair sales made up 35% of dealer turnover in 2025. Dealers surveyed by Artnet News said they make "between 20 and 60 percent of their annual sales" at fairs.

The stand

The big fairs such as Art Basel and Frieze charge by the square metre. Many fairs do not publish their prices, so the best figures come from reporting. In 2018 The Art Newspaper compared estimated prices for a 40 sq m stand in the main section of several fairs. At Fine Arts Paris the estimate was $60,840, all included. At Art Basel Hong Kong it was $34,400 and at Art Fair Philippines $7,600. The cheapest booth at Independent New York was $10,000. The paper noted that many fairs would not give figures, while Tefaf publishes its costs.

More recent prices show how fairs now charge different rates for different stands. At Frieze London in 2025, the main section cost £582 per sq m for stands of 30 to 50 sq m, rising to £658 for the largest stands of 110 to 130 sq m. In 2021 there had been a flat rate of £524. Frieze Masters charged £732 per sq m for every stand, up from £631 in 2021. The Focus section for younger galleries went the other way, from £338 per sq m in 2021 to £276, so a 30 sq m Focus stand cost £8,280 in 2025 against £9,390 a year earlier.

Art Basel's prices are in Swiss francs. When it introduced its new Premiere section in 2025, The Art Newspaper reported that a 32 sq m stand there cost SFr22,000, against almost SFr26,000 for the same size in the main section. A 30 sq m stand in Statements, the section for younger galleries, cost SFr10,000.

Everything else

The stand is far from the only cost. In a survey of dealers, Artnet News found that taking part in Art Basel Miami Beach cost galleries between $120,000 and more than $275,000 once shipping, furniture hire and dinners were included. The main-section booth alone was $50,400 to $100,800, depending on size. Airfare and shipping together "can run around $50,000", and Artnet says these costs are similar whatever the size of the gallery.

Artnet set out two galleries' own estimates. A small gallery with five to ten staff put its costs at $75,000 on the booth, $8,000 on extras, $20,000 on flights and hotels, $15,000 on shipping and $10,000 on entertaining, a total of $128,000, against gross sales of about $2m. A mid-sized to large gallery with 25 to 35 staff put its total at $275,000, with $100,000 for the booth, against gross sales of about $5m. Short-term insurance costs smaller galleries proportionally more, and they may need to hire temporary staff.

Gross sales are not profit. Galleries share the sale price with their artists, and an even split remains common, though it is not a fixed rule. If the small gallery above kept half of its $2m, it would have about $1m before paying the $128,000 fair bill and all its other costs. That is our own illustration, not a figure Artnet reported.

Who loses money

Dealers in the Artnet survey said they failed to make a profit at a fair between 15% and 50% of the time. Olav Velthuis of the University of Amsterdam told Artnet: "People don't realize that fairs are loss leaders for many small galleries." Magda Sawon of Postmasters gallery put the problem plainly: "the ones that make tons of money at fairs are paying the same to participate as those that lose money."

Costs are still rising. The Art Basel and UBS report found that dealers' operating costs rose by about 5% in 2025, faster than the 2% rise in their sales. Dealers surveyed for the previous year's report ranked fairs as their third most concerning cost, Tim Schneider wrote in February 2026. In 2025 The Art Newspaper reported that a dealer who sold nothing in Statements at Art Basel could leave "out of pocket by as much as $20,000", and that a small gallery may turn over less than $200,000 a year. Rózsa Farkas, founder of the London gallery Arcadia Missa, said: "The fairs in the middle are riskier and riskier for galleries." The gallery Clearing chose to skip fairs that year and put on a group show in a rented villa instead.

How fairs have responded

In August 2018 Art Basel and Frieze both announced sliding scales, so that larger stands pay more per square metre. Art Basel's new rates, due to start at the Basel fair in 2019, were to run from CHF 760 per sq m for the smallest main-section stands to CHF 905 for the largest, replacing a flat CHF 830. The Statements fee was to fall from CHF 12,000 to CHF 10,000, and galleries moving up to the main sector would get 20% off in their first year and 10% in their second. Shipping and staff were not covered. The dealer Thaddaeus Ropac called the change "a good start".

Speaking at a London conference in 2018, Jagdip Jagpal, director of India Art Fair, said that supporting younger galleries was "not a charitable act… just good business sense." Frieze London introduced tiered prices in 2024, and sponsors now help some younger galleries: Stone Island has funded a bursary for Focus galleries since 2023, and Tiffany & Co. does the same for the Artist-to-Artist section from 2025. Kristell Chadé, Frieze's executive director of fairs, said the aim is to help galleries make "the step up into the main section." Art Basel's fair director Maike Cruse called the new Premiere section the "sweet spot" that had been missing between Statements and the main section.

What it means

A fair is a bet made in advance. Artnet describes it as paying hundreds of thousands of dollars up front with no guarantee of a payoff. In Artnet's two examples the fair bill came to a similar share of gross sales, around 5% to 6%, but a small gallery has much less room for a bad week. Artnet notes that a small gallery could sell out and still barely recoup its costs.

Dealers still go because a fair can bring more than sales. The San Francisco dealer Anthony Meier told Artnet that the value of a fair "could be on the sell side, the buy side, a referral, or an introduction to an artist." Freddie Powell of Ginny on Frederick put the case for taking the risk: "Yes, every fair is a risk, but if my gallery can't take risks now, when can we?"
