Why Luxury Brands Work With Artists

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

Louis Vuitton has worked with artists on its products for more than twenty years, and other houses have done the same. The terms are private, and what each side gets out of the deals is only partly known.

Murakami and the monogram

One of the best-known partnerships began in 2003, when the designer Marc Jacobs and Louis Vuitton launched a product collaboration with the Japanese artist Takashi Murakami. His reworkings of the monogram ran until 2007, according to The Business of Fashion, though other accounts give later end dates. The same article says Vuitton's work with artists helped turn "a staid French luggage label" into "one of the most valuable fashion brands in the world", and that Murakami "helped to cement Vuitton's new image."

Not everyone in the art world was comfortable. When Murakami's retrospective at the Museum of Contemporary Art in Los Angeles included Vuitton bags for sale, the former auctioneer Simon de Pury recalls, it "made the soi-disant defenders of high art cringe." Vuitton and Murakami worked together again in 2024, two decades after the first pairing.

Other artists followed. Richard Prince worked with the house in 2008, according to nss magazine. In 2017 Jeff Koons reworked masterpieces by Leonardo, Rubens, Fragonard, Gauguin and others for Vuitton's Neverfull, Speedy and Keepall bags. De Pury's verdict: "It was a brand splashing brands on a brand."

Kusama, the largest of them all

In summer 2012 Louis Vuitton launched a collection with Yayoi Kusama that The Business of Fashion called "probably the largest artist collaboration initiated by any luxury goods or fashion house to date." It covered leather goods, clothing, shoes, sunglasses, watches and jewellery. Window displays ran in 460 Louis Vuitton stores in 64 countries, and BoF says the Kusama windows and concept store at Selfridges in London were "the largest single brand takeover the store has ever engaged in."

The deal sat alongside museum work. Vuitton supported Kusama's retrospective at Tate Modern, which BoF says was visited by 186,551 people, and sponsored its tour to the Whitney in New York. The deals were brokered by Glenn Scott Wright, director of the Victoria Miro gallery, working closely with Kusama's Tokyo gallery, Ota Fine Arts. He described two separate contracts, one for the windows and one for the design collaboration with Marc Jacobs.

A second Kusama collaboration followed in January 2023, offering 400 objects. Writing in The Art Newspaper, Georgina Adam wrote that Kusama's "latest works are just a tired reiteration of all that has gone before". She noted that Kusama, then 93, did not seem to have commented on the collaboration herself, and asked who exactly was signing on the "(polka) dotted line". None of the reporting we read answers that question.

What the brands get

For a brand, the attraction is the association. An article in The Conversation notes that "studies have suggested that an association with art allows commercial brands to be perceived as more luxurious", though it does not name the studies. The partnerships also bring attention: BoF reports that during the Kusama launch, the press said Selfridges' concept store repeatedly sold out of pieces.

Sales figures are harder to find. None of the sources we checked gives reliable numbers for the Kusama or Koons lines. A 2017 nss magazine feature says Murakami's line made "above $ 300 million profit only in the first year", but it does not say where the figure came from, so it should be treated with caution.

What the artists get

None of the sources we read says how much any artist was paid. What is on record is exposure. BoF says Kusama appeared on the covers of magazines including Wallpaper, Pop and The Saturday Telegraph Magazine. Glenn Scott Wright says the collaboration "made her into an international household name", and de Pury says it "helped towards establishing her in the art-world firmament."

There are risks too, especially for younger artists. Hervé Mikaeloff, an art consultant to LVMH and the Fondation Louis Vuitton, told BoF: "We have to be careful, particularly with a young artist, that it doesn't affect their career."

Foundations and museums

Some houses have gone further than collaborations and built institutions of their own. Prada opened Fondazione Prada in 1993, and its touring Prada Mode programme brought exhibitions by Damien Hirst to Dubai in 2022 and by Theaster Gates to Abu Dhabi in 2025. The Conversation describes Fondazione Prada and the Fondation Louis Vuitton in Paris as "fully-fledged art galleries", with collections that include Jeff Koons at Prada and Matisse and Warhol at Vuitton. The Vuitton foundation's building was designed by Frank Gehry.

Smaller collaborations are common across the industry. Longchamp worked with Tracey Emin in 2004, Dior's Lady Dior Art project invites artists to reinterpret its best-known handbag, and Gucci has replaced some of its campaigns with digital paintings by Ignasi Monreal and murals by Alex Merry.

What it means

The case for these partnerships is clearest from the brand's side, where the link with art can make a product seem more luxurious. For artists the picture is less clear. The exposure can be large, as Kusama's case shows, but the terms are not made public, and the artist's name ends up on handbags as well as in museums. The Conversation's view is blunt: "it is hard to see that these forays into the art world are about anything more than merely enhancing their own brand."
