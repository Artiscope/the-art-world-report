The Business Case for Art in Luxury Hospitality

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • What hotels say they get
  • How good is the evidence?
  • What it means for artists and galleries

Luxury hotels now talk about art the way they once talked about thread counts. The spending is real. The evidence that it pays is thinner than the enthusiasm suggests.

In London, The Dorchester has spent two years restoring a suite designed in 1952 by the stage designer Oliver Messel, murals and all. Its rate on reopening was set at £15,000 a night, Claire Wrathall reported in The Art Newspaper. Across town a penthouse at Claridge's holds 75 works by Damien Hirst.

These are not isolated gestures. Wrathall notes that 757 five-star-plus rooms were expected in London in 2025 alone. When that many hotels offer the same marble and the same service, art is one of the few things left that a competitor cannot copy. This article looks at how hotels use it, who gains, and how much of the business case has been measured.

An old arrangement

Hotels and artists have traded with each other for a long time. From the 1930s La Colombe d'Or, in the south of France, accepted works from painters in exchange for stays, Maïa Morgensztern wrote for Artnet News. In 1992 Benesse House opened on the Japanese island of Naoshima as a museum and hotel in one building, a model Art Basel's Mahoro Seward treats as a starting point for the modern art hotel.

The commercial version arrived with Soho House, founded in 1995. Morgensztern reports that it set aside £150,000 to £400,000 for art at each venue, bought mostly from emerging artists for £1,000 to £5,000 a work, and often paid in stays, meals and memberships instead of cash.

The relationship has since moved upmarket. In 2018 the owners of the gallery Hauser & Wirth opened their own hotel, the Fife Arms in the Scottish Highlands, which holds about 16,000 works of art and design.

Three ways hotels get their art

The owner's collection. The simplest route is to hang what the owner already has. Seward lists several hotels built around a private collection, from Marrakech to Athens. It costs the hotel little extra and gives the collection an audience.

A partnership with a gallery. Here the gallery supplies the work and the hotel supplies the walls and the guests. Morgensztern found that galleries usually pay for production and installation, and that leading hotels rarely take a share of any sale. For one exhibition at a Paris hotel in 2019, about half of the buyers came through the hotel and half were existing clients of the galleries.

Commissions and licences. Larger groups need art in hundreds of rooms. One answer is to license reproductions. When the Archer hotel opened in New York in 2014, about half of the art budget went to the artists whose images were used, according to the adviser Deborah Goodman Davis, as Rachel Corbett reported in The Art Newspaper. The photographer Thomas Allen said in the same report that his own commission was "not all that much".

What hotels say they get

Hoteliers give three reasons.

The first is distinction. Seward argues that as luxury shifts towards experiences, art helps a hotel stand apart and gives guests a way into the local scene.

The second is attention. Joshua Gardner, who runs Broadwick Soho in London, told Wrathall plainly that "these days you need something for Instagram".

The third is price, and here the evidence weakens. In a 2017 CoStar report, the head of one art agency said he saw a direct link between a hotel's art strategy and the rates it can charge. In the same article a senior Wyndham executive, Brian Watson, admitted there is no formal science behind it.

How good is the evidence?

That admission is worth dwelling on. The figures most often quoted in favour of hotel art come from the people who sell it.

One statistic in the trade press says that 80% of respondents believe the softer benefits of art feed through to revenue and room rates. It comes from a survey by NINE dot ARTS, an art consultancy whose clients are hotel and property developers, and it records what property professionals believe, not what a hotel earned.

We did not find an independent study that isolates the effect of an art programme on a hotel's rates or occupancy. That does not mean there is no effect. It means the case currently rests on judgement and example more than on measurement.

What it means for artists and galleries

For artists the appeal is exposure to people with money and time. Morgensztern cites a hotel owner who bought a work by Titus Kaphar for about $25,000. Five years later, the artist's work was selling for six-figure sums.

There are costs to weigh as well. Payment in kind is not income. A reproduction licence may pay little once an adviser and a gallery have taken their shares. And some artists dislike the setting. Morgensztern recalls that in 1959 Mark Rothko withdrew from a commission for a New York restaurant, reportedly because he did not want his paintings to be something diners glanced at.

For galleries a hotel offers a second showroom in front of travelling collectors. The collector Kenny Schachter told Morgensztern that hotels are putting more interesting work on their walls but have not yet worked out how to sell it.

Questions worth asking

  • For hotels: What is the art for: distinction, press, or sales? Each needs a different programme and a different budget.
  • For artists: Is the fee paid in money? Who owns the image rights? Will the work be shown as art, with a label and a name, or as furnishing?
  • For galleries: Who pays for installation and insurance, and who keeps the buyer's details?
