How Artists Move Between Primary and Secondary Markets

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • The usual direction
  • What galleries do when the work appears
  • Going the other way
  • The houses become dealers, and the dealers become houses
  • What it means
  • Sources

The art market is usually described as two markets with a wall between them. In practice artists, galleries and auction houses cross it all the time, and each crossing changes who is in control.

The textbook version is simple. In the primary market a work is sold for the first time, often consigned to a gallery directly by the artist. In the secondary market a work that someone already owns is sold again, usually through an auction house or a private dealer.

The two behave differently. Karim Crippa, writing for Art Basel, describes primary prices as moving gradually and under control, and secondary prices as driven by bidding and far more volatile. For an artist, the move from one to the other is the point where their prices stop being managed and start being public.

The usual direction

Most artists cross in one direction and do not choose when. A collector who bought from the gallery decides to sell, and consigns the work to auction. The artist is not the seller, so, as Crippa notes, the artist does not usually benefit from any jump in price.

Galleries prefer this to happen late. In a 2019 report for The Art Newspaper, Anna Brady quotes Marc Payot of Hauser & Wirth saying he would have preferred a work by Amy Sherald not to appear at auction yet, because there was still a lot of work to do on her institutional career.

The risk is a weak result. The adviser Lisa Schiff told Brady that an artist can sell well through galleries and still do badly at auction, and that this can leave them with no secondary market at all. Auction results are recorded in price databases such as Artnet's, so a poor one stays visible to every future buyer.

What galleries do when the work appears

Galleries do not simply watch. Brady's report describes several responses.

  • They are told in advance. The New York dealer Marianne Boesky says auction houses often let galleries know when their artists are in a sale.
  • They alert their collectors. One gallery director describes contacting clients who do not normally buy at auction when the estimate looks reasonable.
  • They hold their own prices. A work by Rashid Johnson had made $1.2 million with fees at Sotheby's, yet Hauser & Wirth offered his work at Art Basel for $475,000. Payot told Brady that one strong result is no reason to move gallery prices to that level.
  • They remember who sold. Alex Logsdail of Lisson Gallery says he would not sell again to a buyer who bought a work and put it into auction a year later.

Some go further. Jane Morris, writing in Apollo, notes that well-funded galleries have at times bid at auction to prevent a weak result. Boesky, for her part, says she does not want to hold up prices the market cannot sustain.

Is that kind of bidding allowed? The lawyer Jon Sharples, writing in The Art Newspaper, argues that a dealer acting alone to bid on work by their own artists is unlikely to break UK law, provided nobody is bidding on works they consigned themselves. Coordinated schemes to push up prices are a different matter and may be covered by the law on bid-rigging and fraud.

Going the other way

Occasionally an artist crosses on purpose, taking new work straight to auction.

The best-known case is from 2008, when Damien Hirst sold 223 new works at Sotheby's in London and bypassed his dealers. The two-day sale raised £111.4 million, Anny Shaw reported in The Art Newspaper.

Auction houses have since tried gentler versions that include the gallery. In September 2022 Sotheby's launched Artist's Choice, selling works consigned jointly by artists and their galleries. The six works in that first sale carried a combined estimate of $255,000 to $360,000 and made $919,800 with fees, according to Apollo.

The results were strong, but the idea has not spread. Shaw reported a year later that no further Artist's Choice sales had been held. A similar group of primary works offered at Christie's in the same month did less well, and Morris concludes that most living artists do not have the depth of demand an auction needs.

The houses become dealers, and the dealers become houses

The larger shift is quieter. Auction houses now do a great deal of selling away from the saleroom. Sotheby's reported $1.3 billion and Christie's $1.7 billion in private sales in 2021, Apollo notes. Those are negotiated deals at fixed prices, which is how galleries work.

Some of that is new work. Of the 14 private selling exhibitions Phillips held in 2022, eight featured primary-market works. In August 2023 Phillips also launched Dropshop, a channel for newly commissioned work under which artists receive 3% of resales made through the house.

Galleries cross the other way. Many sell secondary-market works alongside the artists they represent, and a resale handled by the gallery is one it can price and place itself.

What it means

For an artist, the two markets are less a pair of stages than a question of who is selling. When the gallery sells, the price is managed and the artist is paid. When a collector sells at auction, the price is public and the artist usually is not. In the UK the Artist's Resale Right returns a small share.

The experiments of the past few years are attempts to close that gap, by giving artists a seat at the auction or a cut of the resale. None has yet replaced the ordinary route. For most artists the move into the secondary market still happens when somebody else decides it should.
