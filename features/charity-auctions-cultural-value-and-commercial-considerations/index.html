Charity Auctions: Cultural Value and Commercial Considerations

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • What the artist gives

  • What the buyer can claim
  • Models that share the proceeds

Charity auctions raise very large sums and almost nobody criticises them in public. The people who supply the art, and the people who rely on auction prices, have more mixed feelings.

The sums are not small. Sotheby's says its auctions raised more than $430 million for 240 charitable organisations in 2022, The Art Newspaper reported. Christie's put the figure for its own charity auctions at $440 million for the first half of that year alone, according to Melanie Gerlis in the same paper.

Most of that comes from collectors selling whole collections for philanthropic ends. But a steady share comes from a humbler source: living artists who are asked to give a work so that it can be sold for a cause. This article looks at what that exchange is worth to each side.

Why they work

A charity auction does something an ordinary appeal cannot. It offers the donor an object. The adviser Megan Fox Kelly told Gerlis that buyers are mostly chasing the art and not the cause, and that these sales draw money from people who might not otherwise give.

They also draw higher bids. Gerlis notes that buyers tend to pay more for charity lots, and that auction houses sometimes waive or reduce their fees. A small painting by Tracey Emin, And you Kissed me, made £80,000 at Bonhams's Cure3 exhibition for Parkinson's research. The report does not say whether that figure includes fees.

For the charity, then, the case is simple. The questions start with the people who provide the lots.

What the artist gives

An artist who donates a work gives up the income from it. They are also asked often. One artist interviewed for a 2018 report by Amy Costello estimated she received about 50 requests a year, and had paid for framing and shipping on works that then failed to sell.

Saying no is awkward. Anna Brady, writing in The Art Newspaper, wrote that picking holes in the charity auction model is "frankly, taboo", and that the buyer is usually celebrated while the artist is often forgotten. Galleries, which would normally share in a sale, are usually left out altogether.

The tax position can add to the sense of imbalance. In the United States, Costello reports, an artist who donates a work can deduct only the cost of materials, not what the work sells for.

What it does to prices

The commercial effects run in both directions, and both can cause trouble.

Too high. A charity room is generous by design. In 2008 the Red auction at Sotheby's, held for AIDS, tuberculosis and malaria charities, set 17 auction records for artists, Gerlis notes. Such records stay in the price databases. The fact that they were set at a benefit does not.

That matters because a single inflated result can start a run. Katya Kazakina of Artnet News describes one young painter whose work, estimated at $2,000 to $8,000, sold for $94,000 at a benefit. Speculators followed, the artist's auction record reached $317,000, and prices later fell sharply.

Too low. The opposite risk applies at smaller events. The Los Angeles gallery owner Mat Gleason told Costello that collectors use charity sales to buy for far less than a gallery would charge, which gives them less reason to pay full price elsewhere. That is one dealer's view and the report offers no figures to test it, but it is a concern galleries raise often.

Not every charity result enters the record. Kazakina notes that charity lots sold within the major houses' regular auctions are logged in price databases, while auctions run by charities themselves at galas are not.

What the buyer can claim

Buyers sometimes assume a charity purchase carries a tax benefit. In the UK the rules are narrow. Under HMRC's Gift Aid guidance, the buyer is paying for an item. Only the amount paid above the item's known retail value can count as a donation, and bidders must be told that value beforehand.

For something unique, the guidance treats the item's value as whatever it fetches in the auction. Buyers of original art should check with the charity before assuming any part of the price can be treated as a gift.

Models that share the proceeds

Some organisers have tried to rebalance the exchange. The Stand, an online platform launched in 2021 by Robin Woodhead, formerly chief executive and chairman of Sotheby's International,, gives 70% of each sale to the artist and their gallery, 20% to charity and keeps 10% for costs, Brady reported.

Sotheby's took a similar line with Artist's Choice in 2022. Artists and galleries consigned new work to auction, and 15% went to charity, a donation shared between the artist and the auction house. As we noted in our feature on primary and secondary markets, that format has not been repeated.

Questions worth asking

None of this is an argument against charity auctions. It is an argument for setting them up with care.

  • For artists: Is there a reserve, so the work cannot sell for a fraction of its gallery price? Will the gallery be involved? Will I be told who bought it?
  • For charities: Would a share of the proceeds for the artist bring better work, more often? Are estimates in line with gallery prices?
  • For buyers and anyone reading results: Was this price set at a benefit? If so, treat it as a measure of generosity as much as of value.
