The Art Market in 2025: What the Art Basel and UBS Report Found

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

  • Three markets dominate

Global art sales grew in 2025 for the first time in three years. The growth came mostly from the most expensive works, and dealer sales barely moved.

Each spring, Art Basel and UBS publish an estimate of how much art changed hands the year before. The report is written by the cultural economist Clare McAndrew of Arts Economics, and the 2026 edition, the tenth, came out on 12 March 2026. It is also built partly from surveys of dealers, so its numbers are estimates and should be read that way.

This report sets out what it found about 2025, and what the figures do and do not show.

The headline

Sales of art and antiques by dealers and auction houses came to an estimated $59.6 billion in 2025, up 4% on 2024. The number of transactions rose 2% to about 41.5 million.

That ends two years of falling sales, but it is not a return to the peak. Artlyst, summarising the report, puts the 2022 total at $68.1 billion.

Where the money went

The report splits sales between dealers and auction houses.

  • Dealers, including galleries, sold an estimated $34.8 billion, up 2%.
  • Public auctions made $20.7 billion, up 9%.
  • Private sales by auction houses, the deals they broker outside the saleroom, came to just under $4.2 billion, down 5%.

So auctions grew faster than galleries, and within auctions the growth sat at the top. At public auction, the value of lots over $1 million rose 21% and lots over $10 million rose 30%. Below $50,000, both value and volume fell 2%. All ten of the year's most expensive lots sold in New York. The most expensive was Gustav Klimt's Portrait of Elisabeth Lederer, which made about $236 million at Sotheby's.

Tastes at the top also moved back in time. By value, Impressionist and Post-Impressionist sales at auction rose 47% and Old Masters 30%. Contemporary art, which the report defines as work by artists born after 1945, was flat at $1.4 billion. This fits what we described in our feature on auction results for emerging artists: buyers have been retreating from the newest names towards established ones.

Three markets dominate

The United States, the United Kingdom and China together accounted for 76% of sales by value, the same as the year before.

  • United States: 44% of the market, with sales up 5% to $26.0 billion.
  • United Kingdom: 18%, up 2% to $10.5 billion. Public auctions grew, while dealer sales were subdued.
  • China: 14%, with sales up just over 1% to $8.5 billion. Auctions in mainland China strengthened and Hong Kong weakened.
  • France: 8%, up 9% to $4.5 billion, and the largest market in the EU.

Elsewhere the picture was mixed. Switzerland and Austria each grew 13%, while Germany fell 10% and Italy 2%.

A hard year for the middle

The report's dealer survey covered more than 1,650 galleries and dealers in 70 markets, according to Artlyst. It shows a trade that grew on paper while costs rose faster.

Dealers' operating costs went up by about 5% on average, more than their sales. More dealers said profitability had fallen (38%) than said it had improved (33%).

Results depended heavily on size. Dealers turning over less than $500,000 saw double-digit growth in average sales, and those above $10 million grew 3%. Dealers turning over between $1 million and $10 million saw sales fall by 1%. That squeeze on mid-sized galleries is discussed in our feature on how galleries build careers.

Dealers also saw fewer buyers. The average number of individual buyers per dealer fell to 57, the lowest since 2021, and for the smallest dealers it fell 40% to 29. Almost half of buyers (49%) were new to the dealer they bought from.

Art fairs became more important. They accounted for 35% of dealer sales, up from 31% in 2024 and the highest share since 2022.

Online sales shrank

Sales made online fell to $9.2 billion, the lowest since 2019. Online-only sales were 15% of the market, against a peak of 25% in 2020. Online sales still matter for new buyers and lower prices, but the highest-priced lots were sold in live sales.

Women artists

Women made up 45% of the artists represented by dealers, up from 35% in 2018. Their share of dealer sales by value was 37%, up from 28% in 2018. At galleries working in the primary market, women were half of the artists and 44% of sales by value. The gap has narrowed, but it has not closed.

What dealers expect for 2026

Dealers were more hopeful than a year earlier. 43% expected sales to improve in 2026, up 10 points on the year before, and 19% expected them to fall. Among mid-sized auction houses, 21% expected weaker sales, down from 40% a year before.

Reading the figures

The report is useful, but three limits are worth keeping in mind.

  • Dealer figures come from surveys. Galleries do not publish their sales. The dealer figures are estimates based on an annual survey of dealers. The auction figures draw on sales data from auction houses and data providers.
  • A total hides the spread. A 4% rise driven by a small number of very expensive lots says little about the market most artists and buyers deal with. Below $50,000, auction sales fell.
  • One year is not a trend. 2025 was better than 2024, but sales remain below 2022. Whether the recovery holds depends on what happens next.
