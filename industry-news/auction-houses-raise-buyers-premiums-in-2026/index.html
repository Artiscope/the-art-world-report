Auction Houses Raise Buyer's Premiums in 2026

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

In this article

  • Sotheby's, from 13 February
  • Phillips, from 12 April
  • Christie's, from September
  • Bonhams, from 1 October
  • What it means for buyers and sellers
  • Sources

Four of the biggest international auction houses changed what buyers pay in 2026. In each case the top rate on the first part of the price went up.

When a lot sells at auction, the buyer pays the hammer price plus a fee to the auction house, the buyer's premium. The premium is charged in bands: a higher rate on the first part of the price and lower rates above set thresholds. In 2026 Sotheby's, Phillips, Christie's and Bonhams all changed those rates or bands. Here is what changed and when.

Sotheby's, from 13 February

Sotheby's was first. From 13 February 2026 its lowest rate rose from 27% to 28%, and the band it applies to became much wider, The Art Newspaper reported. In London, 28% now applies up to £1.5 million, where 27% used to stop at £800,000. In New York the threshold moved from $1 million to $2 million. The 22% band now runs to £6 million or $8 million, and 15% applies above that, as before.

The Antiques Trade Gazette worked through an example. On a hammer price of £1.5 million, the premium rises from £370,000 to £420,000. The new rates apply across Sotheby's salerooms and categories, except wine and spirits. A Sotheby's spokesperson declined to comment to The Art Newspaper.

Phillips, from 12 April

Phillips announced a new structure for all auctions held on or after 12 April 2026, in New York, London, Hong Kong and Geneva. According to a summary by the law firm Grossman LLP, the rates are 29% up to $2 million, 22% from $2 million to $8 million and 15% above.

Phillips also offers lower rates through what it calls priority bidding. A bidder who leaves a binding written bid at least 48 hours before a live sale, at or above the low estimate, pays a reduced premium if they win. The chief executive, Martin Wilson, said the aim was to encourage early bidding and give sellers more certainty. When the scheme was first announced in 2025, the adviser Todd Levin told Artnet News that bidders should not be penalised for bidding in the room.

Christie's, from September

When Sotheby's made its change, a Christie's spokesperson told the Antiques Trade Gazette that the house had no plans to change its rates. By September it had. Christie's now charges 28% on the first $2 million of the hammer price in New York, 22% up to $8 million and 15% above, MyArtBroker reported. In London the 28% rate applies up to £1.5 million, under a schedule effective from 1 September 2026 in Christie's buying guide. The previous rates, set in September 2025, were 27% up to $1.5 million or £1 million, according to The Art Newspaper.

Bonhams, from 1 October

Bonhams wrote to clients in early September, in a letter published by ArtDependence. For sales after 1 October 2026 the rates are:

  • 30% on the first £25,000 of the hammer price (previously 28%)
  • 28% from £25,001 to £500,000
  • 25% from £500,001 to £1 million
  • 21% from £1,000,001 to £5 million
  • 14% above £5 million

Bonhams said its costs had risen considerably since its last change in July 2023, and that rates on higher-value property had come down. Wine, coins and medals, and motor cars and motorcycles are excluded, as is arms and armour in the US. ArtDependence reported no change to the fee charged to sellers.

What it means for buyers and sellers

For a buyer, the practical effect is that the same hammer price now costs more at lower and middle price levels. At 28%, a £100,000 hammer price costs £128,000 before any tax on the premium. That is why it matters, when comparing results, whether a price is quoted as the hammer or with fees.

Some in the trade argue that sellers end up paying too. MyArtBroker's report quotes the adviser Josh Baer, who says collectors know the total they will spend and will not bid beyond it, so a higher premium means a lower hammer price. MyArtBroker sells art privately and competes with auction houses, so its view is not neutral.
