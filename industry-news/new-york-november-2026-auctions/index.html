New York November 2026 Auctions: Collections, Guarantees and a Van Gogh Record

The Art World Report · 24 November 2026

New York's November sales were carried by private collections, guarantees and a handful of very expensive pictures. A Van Gogh set a new record for the artist after a last-minute settlement over its wartime history.

Twice a year, in May and November, the big auction houses hold their most important sales in New York. Before this autumn's round, The Art Newspaper expected Sotheby's, Christie's, Phillips and Bonhams to bring at least $2.04 billion between them. Below are the main evening sales of 16 to 18 November, as reported by The Art Newspaper. We have not checked each figure against the houses' own lot pages. Prices are given as the hammer price first and then with the buyer's premium, the way the reports give them.

Sotheby's, 16 November

Sotheby's opened the week with two sales: its Now and Contemporary evening auction and the collection of Theodore and Ruth Baum. Together they made $378.7 million with fees.

  • The Baum collection sold every one of its 24 lots, for $90.8 million hammer ($109.9 million with fees). A third of them sold below the low estimate.
  • Yayoi Kusama's No. 3P.B. (1960) made $12.5 million hammer, $15 million with fees, a record for the artist.
  • The top lot was Roy Lichtenstein's Reflections on "Painter and Model" (1990), at $22 million hammer ($24.6 million with fees).

Sotheby's said works from single owners made up almost 60% of the evening's total.

Christie's, 17 November

Christie's 20th-century evening sale made $521 million hammer and $627 million with fees, at the low end of its estimate. Of 75 lots offered, 36 were guaranteed before the sale, 31 of them by outside backers. Six lots did not sell and three were withdrawn.

  • Wassily Kandinsky's Betonte Ecken (1923) made $74 million hammer ($85.8 million with fees), a record for the artist. The seller had bought it at Sotheby's in 1986 for $1 million.
  • Gustav Klimt's Litzlbergkeller (1915–16) made $71 million hammer ($82.3 million with fees).
  • The artist Jeff Koons was among the sellers. Works from his collection made $104.7 million with fees, led by Picasso's Le Baiser (1969) at $26.5 million hammer, well below its $40 million low estimate. Koons had bought it in 2008 for $17.4 million.

Christie's auctioneer Adrien Meyer described "a very calibrated evening with a solid but measured pace".

Phillips, 17 November

Phillips' modern and contemporary evening sale made $85.75 million hammer, $104.4 million with fees, up 55% on the same sale a year earlier. Fourteen of its 22 lots were guaranteed. Amedeo Modigliani's limestone Tête (1911–12) made $38.2 million hammer ($44.6 million with fees). Phillips' chairman Robert Manley said nearly 90% of the works offered had never been sold at auction before.

Sotheby's, 18 November

The biggest night was Sotheby's sale of the Blaquier-Arrieta collection from Argentina, followed by its Modern evening sale. Together they made $663.3 million hammer, $777.8 million with fees.

Vincent van Gogh's Blossoming Chestnut Trees (1890) sold to the dealer William Acquavella for $165 million hammer, $190.4 million with fees. The artist's previous auction record was $117 million with fees, set in 2022. The day before the sale, Sotheby's announced a settlement between the sellers and the heirs of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer, after questions about the painting's history during the Nazi era.

The 12-lot collection as a whole made $412 million with fees against an estimate of about $450 million, with half the lots selling below their low estimates. In the Modern sale that followed, Aaron Douglas's Harriet Tubman (1931) made $12 million hammer ($14.48 million with fees) against an estimate of $2.5 million to $3.5 million, a record for the artist. Mary Cassatt also set a record, at $11 million hammer ($13.3 million with fees) for Summertime (1894–95).

What the week shows

Three patterns stand out from the reports.

  • Collections drive the sales. Single-owner collections, from the Baums to the Blaquier-Arrietas and Koons, supplied many of the top lots, including the two most expensive of the week.
  • Guarantees are everywhere. At Christie's nearly half the lots were guaranteed, and at Phillips almost two thirds. A guaranteed lot sells whatever happens in the room, so high sell-through rates say less about demand than they seem to.
  • Estimates were often generous. Many top lots sold at or below the low estimate. The totals were large, but buyers did not chase every work.

As the Art Basel and UBS report showed for 2025, growth at auction is concentrated in the most expensive works. One week in New York tells you about that top tier. It says little about the market for most artists.
